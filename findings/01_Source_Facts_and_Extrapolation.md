# Departure (2013) — Source Facts & Extrapolation

Analysis of the two existing episodes only. No Tepenian-universe interpretation yet —
this file is strictly about what the original 2013 ASMR series itself establishes,
so that any future reinterpretation has a solid, honest foundation to build from.

Sources: `subs/tactiq-free-transcript-The-Departure-Part-1-oapgiZc5i-g.txt` (Ep. 1,
"Departure (or 'Space Travel Agent')") and `subs/tactiq-free-transcript-The-Departure-Part-2-YYpI8847naw.txt`
(Ep. 2, "Frontiers") — the two cleanest full transcripts of the pair, cross-checked
against the raw YouTube-caption files in the same folder.

Confidence tiers, per the requested method:
- **ABSOLUTE** — stated directly and unambiguously on-screen.
- **PROBABLE** — not stated outright, but a near-certain inference from what is.
- **EXTRAPOLATED** — a reasonable extension built on the above, going past what the text actually supports.

---

## 1. The Company

**ABSOLUTE**
- The agency is named **Departure Space Tours**.
- It sells vacation packages in two broad tiers: **"inner solar"** (destinations within
  the home solar system) and **"exo"** (destinations outside it) — exo is explicitly
  described as longer and more expensive.
- Currency is called **"units."**
- Confirmed package menu (Ep. 1):
  - **Europa** — a 2-day/2-night getaway, 1,200 units, includes stay at partner
    underwater hotels (named example: "the Grand Oceanic Hilton").
  - **Mars** — 4 days/3 nights, "rustic," 1,500 units, includes group archaeological digs.
  - **Gliese 581g** — 22 light-years out, 1,800 units for round-trip fare plus one
    discounted local safari-tour voucher; no hotel included.
  - **Andromeda** and **Triangulum** (galaxies, not star systems) are both named as
    real, nominally bookable — but priced entirely outside this customer's budget.
- Closing ritual: the agent thanks the customer by name-checking the company, and
  asks them to rate the customer service experience — a completely mundane,
  loyalty-program-style transaction, tonally identical to a real-world travel agency call.

**PROBABLE**
- The booking agent (Ep. 1) and the in-flight attendant (Ep. 2) are very likely the
  same *kind* of staff — quite possibly literal **Sherry 3000** android units (see
  §3). This is never stated on-screen; it's inferred from the ad for Sherry 3000
  being placed as the literal cold-open of Ep. 2, immediately before the attendant
  scene, and travel/hospitality/customer service being explicitly named as Sherry
  3000 deployment sectors in that same ad.

**EXTRAPOLATED**
- This is a thoroughly mature, fully commercialized spacefaring economy where
  interstellar tourism is a mundane consumer product, not a rare feat — the
  bureaucratic sales-pitch tone (upsells, discount vouchers, satisfaction surveys)
  only makes sense in a setting where this kind of travel is routine infrastructure,
  gated by cost and queue capacity rather than by technological rarity.
- Wealth stratification is stark and visible to the customer themselves: cheap,
  standardized nearby packages vs. a single, barely-affordable frontier package vs.
  aspirational, priced-out-of-reach destinations (Andromeda/Triangulum) that exist
  openly on the menu as a reminder of what's beyond this customer's means.

## 2. Travel Technology & Procedure

**ABSOLUTE**
- Booking requires a **retinal/biometric scan**, used for three distinct purposes
  across the two episodes: identity verification/background check, accessing
  "tier 1 medical history," and accessing "unit credit" / financial accounts.
- People possess integrated **"nanobiology"** that requires periodic "inoculation"
  upgrades; this upgrade is legally **mandatory specifically for Exosolar Travel**
  (not for inner-solar trips, as far as stated).
- All exo travelers are placed into **sleep stasis** for the full duration of the
  trip — framed by the agent as both a comfort measure and a way to "expedite" the
  trip subjectively.
- In flight: passengers ride in **cryo beds**; ship departs Earth's atmosphere;
  stated cruising speed of **50,000 mph**; cabin is a **zero-gravity environment**;
  magnetic **"Rus" boots** (self-adjusting, one-size-fits-all) are required for any
  movement off the bed, working only on designated paths marked in white light,
  by interacting with the ship's electromagnetic field.
- The key waypoint is the **Port of Phobos** (Mars's moon) — described as the
  longest single leg of the trip out from Earth. Ships queue there 2-3 days for
  access to something called the **"Bifrost Bridge"** — once a ship enters the
  Bridge, the actual interstellar leg to the destination system is "only moments
  away." This is the setting's effective FTL mechanism, and it is explicitly a
  shared, queued, capacity-limited piece of infrastructure at a single chokepoint —
  not a capability every ship has independently.
- A second retinal "barrel scan" links the passenger's nanobiology to the ship's
  monitoring systems; the ship's system can detect trends in the passenger's mood
  over time (implying persistent, cross-visit health/emotional data). An optional
  **"mood upgrade"** is offered onboard, priced 100-300 units by "degree of emotion."
- Stasis induction sequence, in order: guided 4-3-8 breathing (pre-oxygenation +
  natural sedative) → attachment of the **"urchin"** (an arm-mounted feed device
  delivering calories, protein, sugar, fat, vitamins, minerals, *and* the sleep
  agent) → fitting of the **"aora"** (a self-adjusting, head-worn transcranial
  muscle stimulator / sleep-cycle manager / stress monitor, worn to prevent muscle
  atrophy during stasis) → optional **dream-simulation** selection → a final
  retinal scan specifically to lower beta-wave activity for a smooth transition →
  an audible countdown from 25.
- Dream simulations are an **included, optional add-on service**: personalized
  narrative experiences delivered "in volumes" throughout the stasis period, each
  with a "predetermined" outcome (as distinct from a natural dream). Three sample
  offerings are pitched by genre category:
  - *Adventure* — **"The Value of X"**: the sleeper becomes an "exotic matter
    tycoon" commanding fleets and private moons; explicit in-universe tagline —
    **"the one who controls the exotic matter controls the galaxy."**
  - *Mystery* — **"The Dark Wood"**: waking lost at the edge of a dark forest,
    uncertain if it's even one's home world.
  - *Romance* — **"The Garden of Omicron"**: arriving at a welcoming utopian
    society, falling in love with a native, with the narrative tension being the
    risk of forgetting "the vital mission that brought you there."
  - The agent can randomize the selection if the passenger doesn't choose.

**PROBABLE**
- **"Exotic matter"** (mentioned only inside the throwaway dream-sim ad copy) is
  very likely not a random flavor word — "exotic matter" is real-world physics
  jargon specifically associated with theoretical traversable-wormhole/warp
  technology, which lines up suspiciously well with a setting that already has a
  named "Bridge" technology. Reads like an intentional wink at what actually
  powers the Bifrost Bridge, dropped through an in-universe ad rather than
  stated directly.
- The nanobiology/retinal-scan system is probably not exotic to this trip — it
  reads as baseline civilian infrastructure (ordinary people evidently already
  have nanobiology, financial accounts, and medical records all linked to their
  own retina) rather than something installed specifically for space travel.

**EXTRAPOLATED**
- This is a setting where privacy has been substantially traded away for
  convenience: identity, medical history, and finances are all keyed to a single
  biometric credential, and travel data appears to persist and accumulate over
  time (mood-trend detection implies a standing profile, not a one-time intake
  form). A society this integrated would also make it extremely hard for any one
  person to genuinely disappear — which becomes narratively important in §4.
- The Bifrost Bridge being a single, queued, shared chokepoint (rather than
  ship-agnostic FTL) is exactly the kind of infrastructure that would attract
  political control, smuggling, surveillance, and sabotage — a natural pressure
  point for exactly the kind of plot Ep. 2 turns out to contain.

## 3. Sherry 3000 (in-universe advertisement, Ep. 2 cold open)

**ABSOLUTE**
- **Sherry 3000** is a commercial product line of **android laborers**, marketed
  directly to businesses as an "Android Workforce Solution."
- Explicit sales pitch: eliminates the need for lunch breaks, sick days,
  retirement plans, and salaries; operating cost is limited to "maintenance,
  upgrades, and low energy bills."
- Sherry 3000 units work **22 hours a day, every day**, requiring only a 2-hour charge.
- Built from **"synthetic biomaterials,"** and programmed to express the "six
  basic human emotions" — with the ad explicitly stating this comes **"with the
  absence of actual human emotions."** This is a direct, on-screen claim by the
  in-universe advertiser, not an authorial aside — worth flagging as a claim made
  by a company trying to sell a product, not necessarily a neutral setting fact.
- Deployed across many industries, explicitly named: **travel, hospitality,
  customer service, medical, "pop music," and more.**
- Slogan: **"Sherry 3000 — even better than the real thing."**

**PROBABLE**
- (See §1) — the travel-agency staff encountered in both episodes are probably
  Sherry 3000 units, or a closely related product line, given the ad's explicit
  listing of "travel" and "customer service" as deployment sectors and its
  placement directly before the attendant scene.

**EXTRAPOLATED**
- Given the ad is the very first thing the audience hears in Ep. 2, right before
  meeting an attendant who behaves with warm, practiced, almost-too-smooth
  customer-service affect, the juxtaposition plays as a pointed authorial choice:
  inviting the audience to watch the attendant's "care" through the lens of "an
  ad just told me this isn't real." Whether that's meant to be true, ironic, or
  itself part of a larger reveal (never resolved, since Part 3 never happened) is
  a genuinely open question the original creator left hanging.
- Widespread displacement of human labor by androids marketed as sub-human/
  emotionless implies a setting with real, probably unresolved, social tension
  around automation — a second axis of inequality running alongside the
  wealth-based one in §1.

## 4. The "Doc" Plot (Ep. 2, the stasis-dream sequence)

This is the single biggest discovery of this pass: Ep. 2 is not just an ASMR
service scene like Ep. 1 — it has a real embedded plot with a cliffhanger ending,
which is presumably what Part 3 was going to pay off.

**ABSOLUTE**
- Once the passenger is under, an unknown figure breaks into their dream via the
  hacked delivery channel and addresses them as **"Doc."**
- The intruder states, as fact within the fiction:
  - Doc invented a technology that **"deciphered human consciousness"** and made
    it **"tangible"** — something that "could be worked with," "could be uploaded."
    A working consciousness-transfer/mind-upload technology, in other words.
  - Doc's creation was taken from them by an unnamed, more powerful faction the
    intruder calls **"they"**/**"their precious Elite."** This faction hid the
    technology rather than sharing it "with the masses" the way Doc intended —
    they meant to extend human life, "Eternal even," and instead it was
    suppressed and "saved... for their precious Elite."
  - There is an organized **"we"/"our kind"** resistance movement that has been
    searching for Doc "for weeks," and used Doc's own flight booking as their
    opportunity to make contact.
  - Doc is **the only passenger** on this commercial exo-solar flight — presented
    by the intruder as itself proof that "they" are also tracking Doc.
  - The Elite's plan, per the intruder, is to **destroy this specific ship** once
    it crosses into **"dead space"** — i.e., outside any planetary jurisdiction —
    specifically so the destruction carries no legal/political consequence. This
    directly implies **jurisdictional space law exists** and meaningfully
    constrains where an act like this is treated as consequence-free.
  - Doc is given a binary choice: use their own consciousness-transfer technology,
    right now, inside the dream, to leave their dying body and join the
    resistance's effort to reclaim the technology — or refuse, and die with the
    ship regardless.
  - Doc chooses the upload. The episode ends mid-transfer ("say goodbye to your body").
- The line **"I hardly need to explain physiology to a doctor"** confirms "Doc" is
  not just a nickname of convenience — the character has genuine medical/
  scientific credentials.

**PROBABLE**
- Doc's original consciousness-transfer breakthrough was probably developed for,
  or in partnership with, whatever powerful institution later betrayed them — the
  intruder's framing ("your work was theirs and you were done... pushed out and
  forgotten") reads as a funded-researcher-betrayed-by-patron backstory rather
  than a lone inventor being robbed outright.
- The unnamed "Elite" antagonist is probably a governing or corporate power, not
  a purely private one — and given Ep. 1 already establishes a powerful
  regulatory "Republic" that restricts trade and movement around Gliese 581g (see
  §5), there's a real possibility the two were meant to connect, even though nothing
  in either episode states this outright.
- The dream-intruder is probably a trained specialist working for an organized
  operation, not a lone actor — their confident references to "we've been
  keeping an eye on you for a while" and "we've done our part to keep them
  looking over their shoulders" imply prior successful actions by the same group.
- The resistance's motive is probably at least partly personal, not purely
  ideological — describing the stolen breakthrough in terms of "our kind" and
  "all hope isn't lost for our kind" suggests people who see themselves as
  excluded from (rather than just morally opposed to) whatever the Elite are
  doing with the technology.

**EXTRAPOLATED**
- Given both episodes independently establish a powerful authority that hoards or
  restricts something valuable (Ep. 1: political/trade control over a planet;
  Ep. 2: suppression of a life-extending technology), a real Part 3 was very
  likely building toward tying these two threads together — most plausibly by
  revealing that Gliese 581g's "recent conflicts" and restricted provinces are
  themselves a consequence of the same power structure Doc's resistance is
  fighting, turning the destination itself into the actual site of the unresolved
  conflict rather than a coincidental backdrop.
- The choice structure Doc is put through (die attached to a body vs. survive by
  abandoning it) sits close to the same territory as the dream-simulation ad
  moments earlier — "The Garden of Omicron" literally poses "your love or your
  destiny," and "The Value of X" is about who controls a galaxy-defining
  resource. It's a reasonable read that the whole dream-sequence menu was placed
  deliberately as foreshadowing/thematic mirroring of the real choice Doc is
  about to face, even though this is never confirmed.

## 5. Gliese 581g & Political Setting (Ep. 1)

**ABSOLUTE**
- Gliese 581g orbits the red dwarf star Gliese 581, is **22 light-years** from
  Earth, and is **tidally locked** — one permanent-daylight side, one
  permanent-night side, connected by a 24-hour interside shuttle service.
- The planet has recently experienced **"conflicts"**/**"political turmoil."**
- Travel there requires a background check specifically because of this, before
  a visitor is cleared to enter the planet's **"unrestricted provinces"** — which
  implies the existence of restricted/unsafe provinces as well.
- A governing authority called (as transcribed) the **"Google Galactic
  Republic"** imposes export sanctions on certain restricted items native to the
  planet, and maintains an approved-destinations map for tourist travel. A
  reference URL is given: `ggr.gisa581g.fish`.

**PROBABLE**
- "Google Galactic Republic" is almost certainly meant to be heard/read as
  **"Galactic Republic"**, with "Google" functioning as a corporate-branding gag
  (a real-world tech company's name fused onto a governing body) rather than a
  literal in-universe proper noun meant to be taken at face value forever. Worth
  treating as a placeholder/joke name rather than "true" future-history canon.

**EXTRAPOLATED**
- The pairing of recent political conflict + travel restrictions + export
  sanctions on an entire planet, introduced in Ep. 1 as scene-setting texture
  and then never explained, was very likely meant to pay off later — especially
  given the Doc plot in Ep. 2 revolves around exactly the kind of power struggle
  (a controlling authority vs. an excluded population/movement) that would
  explain a planet's sudden unrest.

## Open Threads (things Part 3 never resolved)

- What actually happens to Doc mid-transfer, and whether "leave the body" in
  this technology functions as a clean success, a partial/degraded transfer, or
  something in between.
- Whether the ship really is destroyed, and whether the "commercial exo-solar
  flight with one passenger" detail was itself a giveaway the *audience* was
  meant to catch on Ep. 1's rewatch (a single-passenger flight to a
  politically unstable planet, right after an agent runs an unusually invasive
  background check).
- Whether Gliese 581g's unrest and the Doc/Elite conflict were the same
  conflict all along.
- Who or what the "Elite" actually are, structurally — a corporation, a
  government, both fused together (per the "Google Galactic Republic" gag),
  or something else.
- Whether the travel-agency staff are truly Sherry 3000 units, and if so,
  whether the "absence of actual human emotions" claim was ever meant to be
  taken at face value.
